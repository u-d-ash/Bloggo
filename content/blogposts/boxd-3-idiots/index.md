I went for this movie with my parents as a six-year-old kid. Of course, I don't remember the emotions I went through during the runtime, but what remains is visuals of a deserted Gaurav Path in Surat on a cool and slightly misty winter night after we came out of the hall; it was a late-night show. The movie swept the country on the back of the buttcheek props, "Zoobie Doobie" dominating 9XM over weekends, and of course, Chatur's speech, which was described as the funniest act among the adults in my family. Back then, I didn't get what was so *chamatkari* about it.

Fast forward around 17 years. I rewatch it for the nth time today, on a 5th September, unplanned. I am an "engineer" now, or more precisely, I have an engineering degree from one of those premier institutes on which the movie is based. This is not a critical examination of the flick from a filmmaking perspective (this is not some arthouse cinema here), but rather a reflection on how my thoughts have evolved regarding the ideas presented.

I believe that, apart from my love for solving math problems in my school days, this movie might have influenced me, to some extent, to pick up engineering after school. Before I got engaged in the entrance exam grind, I used to think that having a couple of "Ranchos" beside you in a classroom would definitely be fun if I made it through. And luckily, I did, just to realise that...

> *Kuch baat thi usme. Saala duniya ke taur tarike kadam kadam par challenge karta tha.*

Rancho is a myth. Ranchos don't exist in real life, as far as my experience goes. Now, this doesn't mean that the crowd was void of smart people. I met dozens of "smart" people over my four years, and I don't mean grade-grubbers here. These people were naturally talented; they'd pass the toughest of courses much more effortlessly than the rest. However, if one was to count the number of people among these who had extreme passion for their subject, the fingers should've been enough. In my opinion, these selected few came close to Rancho, but they lost points when it came to the "indisciplined" aspect of the character — his slyness, his audacity and his general philosophy of toying with his life. A friend of mine is the most Rancho-esque person I met, but unfortunately, he has been acting as a "creatively cessated corporate corpse" for a while.

> *Kyu mere dad ke piche pade ho huh?!* \
\
> *Kyuki woh college nhi, factory chala rhe hai. Jisme har saal gadhe manufacture hote hai. Woh dekho woh. Tumhara gadha!*

The dispassionate smart people are your Suhases, strictly in terms of the kind of monetary success they'll eventually find. What I mean is that they'll be wearing hand-stitched genuine Italian leather shoes worth $300, but you will never get a sniff about it; most of them were super humble, whereas Suhas was a pretentious snob. Suhas was the most generically written character of them all because, after spending a few months in Gurgaon, he represents a large chunk of the elite over here.

A few days ago, I came across a social media post defending Suhas on how only "he knew the value of the watch because he had worked hard for it and how his reaction was valid and bla bla bla...". Bullshit. Suhas was a complete ass (as mentioned in the movie), and this shows up in little moments throughout — the altercation with Pia at Connaught Place, and how he refers to her as "Suhas ki fiance ko dekho, doctor banne wali hai..." (notice how he reduces her). I don't mind showing him as a loser.

> *Mere wife ko nhi, uske piche ka bungla dekho idiots. $3.5 million.* *sniggers*

We don't see a lot of him, but, even if we subtract the rote work ethic and the porn magazine distribution sidequest, Chatur was definitely not a saint. I wanted to believe that the reason he looks down on Rancho was because he had been unnecessarily wronged by him in the past, but even before the speech fiasco happens, he had this smug look on his face which shouted "I am so much better". Him and Suhas were crops from the same field.

> *Abba nahi manege..*

Thank you for the meme, Farhan. His character represented the vast majority of us — the ones who don't know what they are doing in life. He was better than the average crowd in the sense that he had a calling, that he pursued and excelled in. The majority of us, including myself, didn't have anything like that. And I think, that is why I spent four years in my college fooling around trying to find my passion because I was quick to realise that, high-level mathematics definitely was not it. This blogpage is one such endeavour. Currently, I am looking for an "Andre Istevan" to complete my arc.

> *Kyuki mujhe meri family ko support karna hai. Maa ki aadhi salary baba ki dawai mai jaati hai...*

We don't call them the average Joe here. We call them the average Raju. The fear that comes along with the uncertainty of the future is very real. And I don't speak this with the engineering college setting in mind, but it's a general principle on which the universe operates. Raju was rather an extreme example for this fear, and it made sense because he did not have a cushion to fall back on had he "failed" in his life.

> *Bachpan se bright student tha. Ma baap ko laga yeh hamari garibi mitayega...*

The pressure of expectations is a difficult thing to handle. I have experienced it multiple times myself, more so after I was tagged with one of these prestigious institutions:

> *Where will you be interning again?* \
> *Adobe.* \
> *So, you did not get into Google or Microsoft?*

> *Since, you're from an IIT, we know that you are smart. That's why you made it there. And we need that smart brain for our work here. That's why we have hired you.*

Every now and then, we read a headline of a suicide in an IIT. In every other case, people like to pull out various explanations: "The Prof. mistreated him." "He had a lot of backlogs." "They did not accept his thesis", and the list is long. Over time, I have come to realise — and the movie captures this idea perfectly — that it's the pressure of expectations that crushes a person's mental state completely.

* Joy's dad was expecting his son to graduate soon and was planning on bringing his entire village. Joy failed him.
* Raju had his family's expectations and did not want to betray his dear friend.
* Virus' son was burdened by his dad's expectation for him to get into a premier engineering college.

Maybe, the root cause of all these student suicides is indeed the pressure of expectations. Consider yourself privileged if you have never felt the pressure of expectations. The movie was quick to label "suicides" as "murders" here. I half agree with the writers here.

Joy's suicide was portrayed as unnecessarily dramatic; I am on Virus' side with that one.

> *Wese life me kai pressures aayege. Toh hamesha tum doosro pe dosh daloge.*

Virus was on point here. Life is not a bed of roses. The events leading to Joy's suicide did not make me sympathise with him but rather pity him on how weak he turned out to be. I think it was a case of sloppy writing, but a deferred graduation as the sole reason for him to take his life didn't make sense.

However, when Virus' son's backstory is revealed to the audience through the medium of the moist blue eyes of a fierce and emotionally charged unspectacled Pia, who ends her monologue with the clichéd punchline of *"Woh suicide nhi, murder tha"*, I am forced to agree with her. I don't find it hard to believe that Virus might've been a terrible father.

> *Matlab, ladka hua toh engineer aur ladki hui toh doctor...*

The curse of the engineer/doctor duopoly has been plaguing our country for decades now, and the reason it still does is because of poverty. The top reason millions of students compete to join the IITs is because of the tales of lucrative pay packages which have been spun by the coaching mafia. And this leads me to believe that the day is not far when the country will completely strip its engineers of any real curiosity. I have seen the symptoms of this myself.

*"Yha pe koi naye ideas ki baat hi nhi karta hai Sir."* still holds (I love that monologue and the classroom scene which follows). Most admits these days have a capitalistic mindset, or as the movie states it, *"...life bas ek profit-loss statement hai..."*, which completely defies the spirit of these great institutions. TL;DR, Mona's delivery wouldn't have succeeded if it was an average IIT hostel in 2026.

There are a lot of weak areas in the plot to point out as well. Raju got the job, and the recruiter was plot armor — or was it really that easy to get a job with an engineering degree back then? And he became some researcher within ten years who owns a villa in Vasant Vihar? Pia and Rancho's romance was quite underdeveloped as well; is "Zoobie-Doobie" a magical spell which causes the girl to yearn for ten years?

The driving force of the movie is obviously the comedic setups which are seamlessly interwoven in the satire. More importantly, they still haven't lost the charm for me. The album is one hit after another. And the mostly-predictable plot twist was a nice way to round the story off. Still an easy 5/5 movie.
